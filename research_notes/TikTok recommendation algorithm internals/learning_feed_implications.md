# Short-form recommendation loops applied to language learning: evidence, level estimation, reading-rate norms, idle detection, and guardrails

Scope: notes for designing a "generated feed" where each card is an LLM-generated item under ~60 s at the learner's level, and the signals are view/reading time plus a four-level grade (Again/Hard/Good/Easy). State of knowledge as of 2026-10-14. Every finding carries a source; anything I could not source is listed under Gaps.

## Key Question 1: What does published research say about learning gains from short-form, swipe-based content versus longer sessions?

### Takeaway
Segmenting *structured* instruction into ~1-minute units is at worst neutral and sometimes positive for retention, but *social-media-style* fragmentation (rapid context switches, filler between clips) measurably hurts both immediate recall and next-day retention; heavy short-form consumption is also correlationally linked to weaker attention and inhibitory control. The learning benefit comes from spacing, retrieval, and comprehensible input, not from the swipe format itself.

### Cited Findings
- Randomized study (68 university students, ages 18–24): four 1-minute videos vs. one 8-minute video with identical content. Short-form "produced higher engagement and satisfaction ratings, particularly among those who self-identified as significant users of video centric social media platforms," but retention on an immediate post-test and a one-week delayed test was **not statistically different** between conditions — [McKeown 2026, IATED](https://library.iated.org/view/MCKEOWN2026DES)
- Wei et al. 2026 (Communications Psychology): three experiments (n = 180 incidental learning, n = 185 intentional learning, n = 59 fMRI). A continuous 10-minute documentary vs. the same content cut into 5–7 clips interspersed with non-informative filler (identical word count and facts). Short-video viewers scored lower on the immediate quiz in both experiments, and the gap **widened the next day** — short-video participants "forgot a much higher percentage of their initial knowledge overnight." fMRI showed inter-subject brain synchronization only in regions handling "immediate, automatic auditory processing," not memory/narrative-integration regions. Caveats: healthy college students only; scanner prevented natural scrolling — [PsyPost summary of Wei et al. 2026](https://www.psypost.org/the-illusion-of-learning-from-short-videos/)
- Meta-analytic evidence on short-form video use and cognition: 71 studies, 98,299 participants; more short-form video use associated with poorer cognition overall (r = −.34), with attention (r = −.38) and inhibitory control (r = −.41) showing the strongest associations. Authors call for longitudinal/experimental work because direction of causality is unclear — [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/); related systematic review preprint — [medRxiv 2025.08.27.25334540](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.08.27.25334540v2.full-text). (I could not verify from the fetched pages which of these two documents the 71-study / r-value numbers belong to; treat the venue attribution as unverified.)
- Microlearning in higher education generally: a systematic review and meta-analysis of microlearning vs. macro-learning retrieved 602 studies, included 12 in the review and 5 in the meta-analysis, reporting positive effects on academic performance — [BMC Medical Education 2022](https://bmcmededuc.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12909-022-03726-8/tables/1); a 2025 systematic review proposes a framework and reports positive effects on knowledge, retention, and engagement — [PMC11774797](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11774797/); a geriatrics-medicine meta-analysis reported SMD 0.80 (95% CI 0.49–1.10) for delirium knowledge — [Otago](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Microlearning-to-teach-geriatric-principles-in/9926865857801891)
- TikTok as a course channel (213 nursing students, two Spanish universities, Feb–May 2022; teachers posted ≥4 videos/week): high acceptance (perceived usefulness 5.38/7, ease of use 6.07/7, behavioral intention 5.09/7; content videos rated 4.37/5), but **no control group and no learning-outcome measure**; authors flag that unreviewed materials "could lead to confusing knowledge processes" — [Fiallos Toscano et al., PMC10235824](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10235824/)
- Language-specific TikTok studies are mostly small, self-report, non-randomized classroom studies in regional journals; they consistently report that on-screen text, repetition, and visual-gesture pairing feel most helpful for vocabulary, and that TikTok lacks depth for grammar/writing and should be a supplement — [example: IJRISS 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bcp/journl/v9y2025issue-3p4729-4738.html)
- Interspersing questions inside short instructional videos improves short- and long-term recall in experimental studies (retrieval practice embedded in the video) — [Sydney IISME](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IISME/article/view/21175)
- "Desirable difficulties": "current performance ... is an unreliable index of learning"; manipulations that speed acquisition can fail to support long-term retention, while spacing, interleaving, variation, intermittent feedback, and testing-as-learning slow acquisition but enhance retention and transfer — [Bjork & Bjork](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_inpress.pdf)
- Distributed (spaced) practice beats massed practice across 254 studies / 839 assessments; optimal gap scales with the retention interval — [Cepeda et al. 2006, Psychological Bulletin](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)
- Evidence that short-session app learning yields measurable proficiency: 245 learners who completed Duolingo's A2 ("Basic") English content using Duolingo as their only tool scored on average ACTFL Intermediate High in reading and listening on Avant STAMP 4S, one sublevel above the expected Intermediate Mid — [Jiang et al. 2024, CALICO Journal](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-duolingo-english-courses-in-developing-readi/); summary on [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/results-duolingo-efficacy-studies)
- Existing "TikTok-style learning feed" products exist (e.g., ScrollEd converts PDFs/textbooks into a vertical feed: swipe up = next topic, sideways = deeper on the current topic) but I found no outcome data for them — [ProPakistani, Sept 2026](https://propakistani.pk/2026/09/21/this-app-turns-your-study-material-into-a-tiktok-style-learning-feed/amp/)

### Inferences
- The two controlled experiments agree once you separate *segmentation* from *fragmentation*: coherent 1-minute units with a through-line (McKeown) retain as well as long form; disconnected clips with filler and context switches (Wei) lose retention overnight. A generated feed should therefore keep thematic continuity between adjacent cards (same story, same character, same grammar target) rather than random topic hopping.
- Because retention, not satisfaction, is the goal, every card should contain a retrieval event (the grade itself, a cloze, a comprehension micro-question). Passive viewing with a swipe is the condition most likely to produce the "illusion of learning."
- The correlational attention findings mean a feed that trains fast swiping may be actively counterproductive; limiting the ease of skipping (e.g., a minimum exposure before "Again"/skip is honored) is a defensible design choice.

### Gaps
- No published randomized study of a swipe-based *language* feed with delayed vocabulary/grammar outcomes was found; the McKeown and Wei studies use general (non-language) content.
- No study isolates the effect of the swipe gesture / infinite-scroll interface from the content length.

## Key Question 2: What concrete models do Duolingo and similar apps use to pick the next item's difficulty, and what signals do they weigh?

### Takeaway
Production systems converge on a logistic (IRT/Elo-family) core: P(correct) = f(ability − difficulty), updated online, with a target success rate roughly 70–75%, and a separate memory-decay model (half-life regression / FSRS) for *when* to re-show an item. Correctness and lag time are the primary signals; response time is widely logged but only used as a model input in a few systems (Math Garden's "high speed, high stakes" rule), and Anki/FSRS explicitly ignore it for scheduling.

### Cited Findings
**Duolingo**
- Birdbrain V1: "a flavor of logistic regression inspired by item response theory," modeling "the probability of a person giving a correct response as a function of two variables, which can be interpreted as the difficulty of the exercise and the ability of the learner"; exercise difficulty is a sum of component features (exercise type, vocabulary words, etc.); it "generalizes the Elo rating system" and updates by "one step of stochastic gradient descent." Birdbrain V2 is "a long short-term memory, or LSTM, model, which learns to compress a learner's history of interactions with Duolingo exercises into a set of 40 numbers," updated "within minutes of a learner completing an exercise," with lesson data streamed in chunks because "roughly 20 percent of lessons started on Duolingo aren't completed." "Birdbrain consistently caused both engagement and learning measures to increase." — [IEEE Spectrum (Duolingo authors)](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)
- Birdbrain started shaping lessons in March 2020 and personalized "over 20% of lessons" by October 2020; A/B tests showed it "consistently helped our learners learn more"; it feeds the Session Generator, which chooses "which of all the possible exercises are at just the right difficulty level for this specific learner" — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/learning-how-to-help-you-learn-introducing-birdbrain)
- Secondary sources state Birdbrain targets roughly a 70% success rate when a learner is getting everything right and trains nightly on about half a billion exercises; I could not confirm either number in the primary Duolingo sources I fetched — [TechAhead (secondary)](https://www.techaheadcorp.com/blog/how-duolingo-personalizes-learning/)
- Half-life regression (HLR): memory half-life ĥ = 2^(Θ·x); features x include lag time since last practice, counts of correct and incorrect recalls, and per-lexeme indicators; loss adds a half-life regression term and L2 regularization. On 12M+ sessions HLR reached 0.13 MAE, "nearly half that of the Leitner system"; in A/B tests: +9.5% practice-session retention, +1.7% lesson retention, +12% overall activity. The blog gives no role to response time — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn); paper: [Settles & Meeder, ACL 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf)
- HLR 2026 revisit (Duolingo-affiliated): a mixed-effects HLR whose random effects feed a random forest, using learner characteristics, lexeme properties, and context (e.g., "time interval since the lexeme was last shown"); SHAP analysis found "contextual features and random-effects features" (interpretable as lexeme difficulty and learner ability) most important; no claim about production deployment — [Ilidio et al. 2026, PMLR v339](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v339/ilidio26a.html)
- The HLR dataset includes "response times" alongside correctness for 6k+ users over 30 days, i.e., Duolingo logs response time even where the model does not use it — [Duolingo Research](https://research.duolingo.com/)

**Elo / IRT with response time**
- Pelánek 2016 (Computers & Education): a student's answer is a "match" between student and item; P(correct) is a logistic function of skill − difficulty, with a shifted logistic to account for guessing (probability 1/k for k options). Typical fixed K = 0.4 (citing Antal 2013; Wauters et al. 2012); better to use an uncertainty function U(n) = a/(1 + bn) with n = number of prior answers (e.g., a = 1, b = 0.05), which gives "fast coarse estimates after few answers and stability in the long run." Response time can enter via Klinkenberg et al. 2011's "high speed, high stakes" rule: score depends on correctness and response time t with a time limit d and discrimination a; "stakes are high when a student answers quickly; with increasing response time the score goes towards zero (for both correct and incorrect answers)." The adaptive practice system "uses a target success rate (e.g., 75 %)" and selects questions to keep achieved performance near it — [Pelánek, CAE-elo.pdf](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)
- Math Garden "leverages both accuracy and response time in its assessments"; a dynamic K-factor that raises K during "noticeable upward or downward shifts in ratings" and lowers it otherwise was found "superior over existing K adjustment strategies" in simulation — [Tilburg University](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/balancing-stability-and-flexibility-investigating-a-dynamic-k-val/)

**Knowledge tracing**
- Bayesian Knowledge Tracing models a hidden binary "known" state per skill with learn/guess/slip parameters — [Corbett & Anderson 1995](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821); Deep Knowledge Tracing replaces this with an RNN over the interaction sequence — [Piech et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05908)

**FSRS / Anki (the grading scheme the feed will reuse)**
- FSRS state: Retrievability R = "the probability that the person can successfully recall a particular piece of information at a given moment"; Stability S = "the time, in days, required for R to decrease from 100% to 90%"; Difficulty D = "the inherent complexity of a particular information." Desired retention "between 70% and 97% [is] considered reasonable"; FSRS-6 uses all same-day reviews, older versions only the first of the day — [awesome-fsrs wiki: ABC of FSRS](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/ABC-of-FSRS)
- Anki: "The time taken does not influence scheduling"; answer timing is recorded for statistics only — [Anki manual, Deck Options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)

**Other apps**
- LingQ: level is inferred from a Known Words count with language-specific thresholds; words carry a 0–5 status (0 never seen → 5 known); each lesson displays the percentage of unknown words, and the library has six difficulty bands (Beginner 1 to Advanced 2) — [LingQ support](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/step-8-lingq-statistics); [Refold review](https://refold.la/blog/lingq-review)

**LLM level control (needed for "generate at the learner's level")**
- Most LLMs "generate text at a near-native level of complexity"; in a user study with university learners of Japanese, prompting alone failed but controllable-generation techniques raised comprehensibility for beginners from 39.4% to 83.3% — [Toward Beginner-Friendly LLMs, arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04072v1)
- CEFR-targeted rewriting (C2/C1/B2 → B1/A2/A1) with LLMs has been benchmarked, and evaluation frameworks combine readability prediction, lexical-constraint validation, and syntactic profiling — [arXiv 2409.20246](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.20246); [BEA 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.bea-1.24.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a generated feed, an Elo/IRT learner-ability scalar (per language, possibly per skill) is the cheapest robust estimator: update after each card with an uncertainty-decaying K; use the grade as a 4-level outcome (map Again=0, Hard≈0.33, Good≈0.67, Easy=1, or treat Again vs. not-Again as the binary outcome and use Hard/Easy to nudge difficulty). Item difficulty for a freshly generated card has no history, so seed it from the generation parameters (target CEFR, vocabulary frequency band, sentence length) — exactly the "sum of component features" idea in Birdbrain V1.
- Response time is a usable secondary signal only when the item has a clear expected duration (short cloze, single sentence). Pelánek's rule shows how: treat fast-and-correct as strong evidence, slow-and-correct as weak evidence, and let the evidence weight decay toward zero at the time limit, which also neutralizes idle outliers.
- Keep two models, as Duolingo does: an ability/difficulty model for *what* to generate next and a memory model (FSRS) for *when* to resurface a previously seen word or pattern.

### Gaps
- No primary Duolingo source confirms the 70% target or the half-billion-per-night figure.
- I found no public description of how Clozemaster or Beelinguapp select difficulty.
- I did not find a source quantifying how much response time improves prediction over correctness alone in a language-learning setting.

## Key Question 3: What are reliable reading-rate figures per language/script for L2 learners at different CEFR/JLPT/HSK levels?

### Takeaway
Solid norms exist for L1 adults (English ≈ 238 wpm silent nonfiction; Chinese ≈ 260 wpm ≈ 390 characters/min silent), and the best cross-language anchor is that *time per matched sentence is roughly constant across scripts*. For L2 readers the evidence is coarse: proficient bilinguals read 10–17% slower than in L1, typical classroom L2 readers read at one-half to one-third of L1 rate (roughly 80–150 wpm), and the only level-banded benchmark I found is Shen's L2 Chinese target of 300–499 cpm in year 4 and >500 cpm in year 5. No CEFR/JLPT/HSK-indexed reading-rate table was found.

### Cited Findings
**L1 norms**
- Meta-analysis of 190 studies / 18,573 participants: adult silent reading in English averages 238 wpm for nonfiction and 260 wpm for fiction; most adults fall in 175–300 wpm (nonfiction) and 200–320 wpm (fiction); oral reading averages 183 wpm (77 studies, 5,965 participants); rates are lower for children, older adults, and L2 readers — [Brysbaert 2019, JML](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Same source: reading for *recall* runs around 100 wpm due to regressions and rereading; rates "in less constrained situations are likely to be lower because of mind wandering and task interruptions"; a short-passage study range spanned just over 100 wpm to nearly 400 wpm; Spanish studies averaged 278 wpm (5 studies) vs. English 236 wpm (144 studies) — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf); [BPS Research Digest summary](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/most-comprehensive-review-date-finds-average-persons-reading-speed-slower)
- Chinese (26 studies): mean 260 wpm silent and 152 wpm aloud, computed with a 1.5-characters-per-word conversion (i.e., about 390 characters/min silent; an overestimate where words are longer); newspaper text averages 1.87 characters per word (Yan et al. 2010) — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- Cross-script invariance: matched short texts in English, Finnish, and Mandarin took the same time per sentence despite 1.5 characters/word in Chinese vs. 5.6 letters/word in English (Liversedge et al. 2016); same result for Hebrew vs. English paragraphs (Kuperman et al. 2019) — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- IReST (International Reading Speed Texts): 10 equivalent ~150-word paragraphs translated into ~17–20 languages, normed on 25 healthy native speakers per language (425 subjects total); English norm 228 wpm; English word length 4.3 letters — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf); [Vision Research EU](https://www.vision-research.eu/index.php?id=641); primary: [Trauzettel-Klosinski & Dietz 2012, IOVS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22661485/)

**L2 readers**
- Proficient Dutch–English bilinguals reading half a novel in each language were 17% slower in L2, with child-like eye movements (more and longer fixations, shorter saccades, fewer skipped words) (Cop et al. 2015); Dirix et al. 2019 found 10% slower rates in L2 (174 vs. 189 wpm reading; 50 vs. 54 wpm studying) — [Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)
- "Most L2 learners read slowly, at one-half to one-third the reading rate by L1 readers," with fluent L1 readers at 250–300 wpm; an L2 French group read 79.4 ± 15.4 wpm, rising to 100.8 ± 15.3 wpm after training — [PMC6602001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6602001/table/T1) (attribution of these two figures comes from the search summary; the table page was not fetched in full)
- Japanese university EFL learners in timed/repeated-reading treatments gained 13–27 standard words per minute — [Reading in a Foreign Language, EJ1176224](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1176224.pdf)
- Character count predicts L2 reading time independently of word count, so character-based rates are recommended for L2 measurement — [Reading in a Foreign Language](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/425)
- L2 Chinese (Helen Shen): fourth-year learners "should be able to read 300 to 499 characters per minute," fifth-year learners "more than 500 characters per minute"; "reading speed does not equal reading comprehension" — [University of Hawaii CCLE](https://manoa.hawaii.edu/ccle/2023/01/06/ccle-talk-series-professor-helen-shen-and-chinese-l2-reading-proficiency-building)
- Japanese *listening* pace for level calibration: beginner-level Japanese audio materials run 4.27 mora/s vs. 7.55 mora/s in native conversation (about 57% of native speed) — [SEATJ 2018](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/seatj/2018/conference/29)

### Inferences
- A practical conversion for a 60-second card budget: an L1 adult reads ~240 English words or ~390 Chinese characters per minute; an L2 reader at roughly half L1 speed handles ~100–130 English words or ~150–250 Chinese characters per minute. A card meant to be *read with comprehension* (closer to "reading for recall," ~100 wpm L1) should therefore hold only ~40–80 L2 words / ~80–150 CJK characters for a 30–45 s target.
- Because time per matched sentence is roughly script-invariant, calibrate expected card time on *content* (sentence count, proposition count, target-vocabulary count), not raw word or character count; then compute an observed/expected time ratio as the level signal.
- A per-learner expected reading rate should be learned online (median of observed ratio over recent Good-graded cards), because between-study variance (100–400 wpm on short passages) dwarfs between-level differences.

### Gaps
- No source gives reading rates indexed by CEFR, JLPT, or HSK level; Shen's Chinese benchmarks are by year of university study.
- I did not find a citable native Japanese silent-reading cpm norm (search summaries mention ~800–1000 characters/min for adults but without a verifiable source), nor a Japanese IReST norm.
- The per-language IReST norms (other than English 228 wpm) were not retrievable from the pages I could access.

## Key Question 4: How do apps treat dwell times that are too long (idle), and is there evidence for a specific cutoff like 60 seconds?

### Takeaway
The 60-second figure is an Anki convention, not an empirical finding: Anki caps recorded answer time at 60 s on the assumption the user walked away, and does not use time for scheduling at all. The learning-analytics literature shows session/idle thresholds are arbitrary and materially change results, and recommender research shows dwell time needs debiasing and that neither very short nor very long dwell maps cleanly to interest.

### Cited Findings
- Anki, "Maximum answer seconds": "The default limit is 60 seconds. If you take longer than that, Anki assumes you have walked away from your computer or have been distracted, and caps the recorded time at 60 seconds, so that you don't end up with inaccurate statistics." Also: "The time taken does not influence scheduling." — [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)
- The cap is applied by the client timer from question display to grade press; users who routinely exceed it are advised to raise the limit or simplify cards — [Anki forum thread on the timer limit](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/is-it-possible-to-increase-the-upper-limit-of-the-maximum-display-time-for-ankis-timer/20555)
- Time-on-task in learning analytics is computed from inter-event gaps: "An activity session is defined by a sequence of events with no gap between them lasting more than a predetermined inactivity threshold" — [WeBWorK log study, arXiv 2005.03826](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.03826)
- Kovanović et al. found "time-on-task estimation methods play an important role in shaping the final study results" across online and blended datasets, i.e., the choice of threshold/outlier rule changes which predictors look significant — [Kovanović et al., LAK 2015](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/penetrating-the-black-box-of-time-on-task-estimation/); [follow-up](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/277d3daa-8658-4e24-aaa2-627873ca080e)
- A log-normal mixture model estimates time-on-task from timestamps alone, modelling on-task gaps and off-task (idle) gaps as separate components rather than a fixed cutoff — [arXiv 1805.01819](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01819)
- Dwell time is a better satisfaction proxy than clicks in recommendation (Yahoo), but it must be normalized per item/content type — [MarTech on Yi et al. 2014](https://martech.org/yahoo-scientists-measure-dwell-time-alternative-click-rate)
- Short dwell is ambiguous: "short time might actually equal satisfaction" in some contexts, so short dwell should not be treated as uniformly negative — [arXiv 2012.13992](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2012.13992)
- Hesitation/tolerance (surveys n = 6,644 and 3,864; e-commerce and short-video logs): users commonly linger or keep engaging without interest ("tolerance"), and tolerance behaviours correlate with *decreased* later activity; treating tolerance as distinct from interest improved retention in a field study — [arXiv 2412.09950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09950)
- Duration bias: watch-time prediction "is always biased towards videos with long duration"; Kuaishou's D2Q deconfounds duration and was fully launched in production — [Zhan et al. 2022, arXiv 2206.06003](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.06003); an explainer reports Kuaishou's feed drifted from shorter to longer content over 11 months because the model rewarded longer watch times — [ML Frontiers (secondary)](https://mlfrontiers.substack.com/p/how-long-is-long-duration-bias-in)
- Duolingo streams partial lesson data because about 20% of started lessons are abandoned, so abandonment is treated as data rather than discarded — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)

### Inferences
- There is no empirical basis for 60 s as *the* idle threshold; it is a sensible default for sub-minute items only because it sits well above the expected per-card time. A content-aware rule is better: cap at k × expected time (e.g., 3× the expected reading time, with a floor around 20–30 s and a ceiling like Anki's), and store both the raw and capped values so the rule can be revisited offline (Kovanović's point).
- Capping (Anki) is preferable to discarding when the grade is still valid; discard the *time* signal but keep the grade when dwell exceeds the cap, and never let a capped time count as "slow reader" evidence in the level estimator.
- Visibility/focus events (tab hidden, app backgrounded, no scroll/touch for N seconds) are stronger idle evidence than any dwell threshold; the time-on-task literature's main lesson is to make the rule explicit and test sensitivity.
- Dwell should be normalized per card expected length (D2Q's lesson) or the feed will drift toward longer cards.

### Gaps
- No study was found that validates a specific idle cutoff for language-learning cards, and no app documentation other than Anki's states its cap.
- I did not retrieve the numeric inactivity thresholds commonly reviewed in the time-on-task literature (the EDM 2021 PDF and Kovanović 2015 full text were not readable via fetch).

## Key Question 5: What design guardrails keep a learning feed from degenerating into an entertainment feed?

### Takeaway
Optimize for a learning metric with a difficulty target (≈70–75% success, "i+1"), not for watch time; make the retrieval grade, not the swipe, the primary reward signal; debias dwell by expected card length; keep narrative continuity between cards; and A/B test on delayed recall. Every production system that works (Duolingo, FSRS-based apps, LingQ) exposes or enforces a difficulty/retention target rather than letting engagement pick difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- Comprehensible input: acquisition happens when learners understand input "a little beyond" their current level (i+1) — [Krashen, Principles and Practice](http://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf)
- Adaptive practice keeps achieved success near an explicit target ("e.g., 75 %") — [Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf); FSRS exposes desired retention with 70–97% as the reasonable band — [ABC of FSRS](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/ABC-of-FSRS)
- Duolingo evaluates Birdbrain on both "engagement and learning measures" in A/B tests, rather than engagement alone — [IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo); its HLR launch reported engagement lifts (+12% activity) as the business outcome — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn)
- Performance during learning is a poor proxy for learning; easy, fluent, fast-feeling sessions can reduce retention (desirable difficulties) — [Bjork & Bjork](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_inpress.pdf)
- Fragmented content with context switches lowers immediate and next-day recall; synchronized brain activity confined to auditory regions — [Wei et al. 2026 via PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/the-illusion-of-learning-from-short-videos/)
- Coherent segmented content retains as well as long form while being rated more engaging — [McKeown 2026](https://library.iated.org/view/MCKEOWN2026DES)
- Engagement signals without interest ("tolerance") predict *lower* future activity; modelling them separately improved retention — [arXiv 2412.09950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09950)
- Watch-time objectives drift feeds toward longer items unless duration is deconfounded — [arXiv 2206.06003](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.06003)
- LingQ surfaces "% unknown words" per lesson and a level slider so the learner sees difficulty explicitly — [Refold review](https://refold.la/blog/lingq-review)
- LLMs default to near-native text; CEFR control requires constrained generation and automatic readability/lexical checks, not prompting alone — [arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04072v1); [arXiv 2409.20246](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.20246)
- Heavy short-form consumption correlates with lower attention and inhibitory control (r ≈ −.38 / −.41) — [PsyPost meta-analysis summary](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/)

### Inferences (design guardrails)
1. **Objective function**: rank candidates by predicted learning value (probability of recall in the FSRS/Elo sense, closeness to the ~70–75% success target, novelty of target vocabulary), with dwell used only as a comprehension/level signal after normalization by expected time. Never rank by raw watch time.
2. **Grade before swipe**: require the Again/Hard/Good/Easy grade (or an equivalent micro-retrieval) before advancing; treat an ungraded swipe-away as "Again" or as missing data, not as a neutral event.
3. **Difficulty drift control**: hold the learner-ability estimate in an Elo/IRT model updated by grades (and, optionally, time ratio), generate the next card at ability + small delta (i+1), and bound per-session drift (e.g., no more than one CEFR sub-band of change per session) to avoid runaway easy/hard loops when a learner is tired or distracted.
4. **Expected-time normalization**: compute expected read/view time from the card's content (sentence count, L2 word/character count at the learner's estimated rate, audio duration); use observed/expected ratio, capped, as the signal; cap or discard times beyond k × expected and on focus-loss events (Anki's 60 s is a reasonable absolute ceiling for sub-minute cards).
5. **Continuity**: generate cards in short arcs (same story/topic across 3–5 cards) to avoid the fragmentation effect; vary surface form (interleaving) within an arc rather than hopping topics.
6. **Evaluate on retention**: A/B on delayed recall (next-day, one-week), not only on session length or cards per session; log both so the trade-off is visible, as Duolingo does.
7. **Level-check the generator**: validate every generated card against the target CEFR band with automatic readability and vocabulary-frequency checks before serving, since prompting alone is unreliable.

### Gaps
- No published post-mortem of a learning feed that drifted into entertainment was found; the drift evidence comes from general short-video platforms (Kuaishou) and lab studies.
- No public documentation from YouTube Shorts-based language products, Beelinguapp, or Clozemaster on how they balance engagement and difficulty.
