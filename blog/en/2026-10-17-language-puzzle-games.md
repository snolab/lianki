# Language Puzzle Games: Chants of Sennaar and Other Games Where You Decipher a Language

_Posted October 17, 2026 by [lianki.com](https://lianki.com)_

Most "language games" are flashcards with points attached. The games in this post are something different: they drop you into a world where nobody speaks your language, hand you a page of strange symbols, and leave you to figure it out.

That is, in miniature, exactly what learning a real language feels like at the very beginning. You see a sign, notice a symbol you have seen before, form a guess, and wait for the next clue to confirm or break it. These games turn that process into the whole point — and playing them can make you notice how you actually learn.

Below are six games where deciphering a language is the main mechanic or a central secret, with what you do in each and why it is worth a language learner's time.

---

## 1. Chants of Sennaar

- **Developer / publisher:** Rundisc (France) / Focus Entertainment
- **Released:** September 5, 2023 on Windows, Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 4 and Xbox One; Android and iOS on August 26, 2025
- **Link:** [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1931770/Chants_of_Sennaar/)

**The language mechanic.** Chants of Sennaar is loosely based on the Tower of Babel myth. You climb a tower where each level belongs to a different people with their own culture and their own written language — the Steam page describes "five different interconnected languages." Each one is written with symbols, and you work out what they mean from gestures, illustrated signs, and what is happening around you.

Every new symbol goes into your notebook, where you can type in your guess for what it means. Every so often the notebook gives you a page of drawings — nouns, verbs, adjectives — and asks you to match symbols to them. Only when the **whole page** is right does the game confirm your guesses and show the real meanings. As Mark Brown of Game Maker's Toolkit put it, "you have to correctly fill in an entire page to validate your hunch," so you can't just guess your way through.

Later in the game you also have to carry meaning _between_ languages, acting as the translator between peoples who can't understand each other.

**Why it's interesting for learners.**

- It is the clearest demonstration of **learning vocabulary from context**. You never get a dictionary; every word is earned from a situation.
- The notebook is a deck you build yourself, and the confirmation pages are a quiz — you only "know" a word once you can recall it in a test, not when you first see it.
- The languages are deliberately small. Mark Brown estimates around 30 words per people, which keeps each one learnable. That is a good reminder that a small, well-learned core vocabulary goes a long way.

## 2. Heaven's Vault

- **Developer / publisher:** inkle
- **Released:** April 16, 2019 on Windows and PlayStation 4; Nintendo Switch on January 28, 2021
- **Link:** [Official site](https://www.inklestudios.com/heavensvault/) · [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/774201/Heavens_Vault/)

**The language mechanic.** You play Aliya, an archaeologist, who collects inscriptions from artifacts and ruins and translates the hieroglyphic language of a lost civilization called the Ancients. The developers say more than a thousand words of "Ancient" can be found in the game, and its pictorial glyphs draw on Ancient Egyptian and Chinese writing.

You translate mostly by inferring meaning from context. Once you have a word, it comes back again and again: a word you learned on one artifact turns up inside a new phrase, or next to a related word on another. Your translations also shape the story — PCWorld's review gives the example of reading a doorway inscription as "Temple": Aliya will call the building a temple from then on, while a different reading would have her call it something else, such as a graveyard. As inkle puts it, a wrong translation "might send you down the wrong track."

Chants of Sennaar's developers have said Heaven's Vault was one of their inspirations.

**Why it's interesting for learners.**

- Heaven's Vault is built on **seeing the same word again in a new place**. That is the core idea of spaced repetition, and of why reading lots of real text matters: each new encounter strengthens what you already half-know.
- It shows that early guesses are allowed to be wrong. Like a real learner, you hold a working hypothesis, keep reading, and revise it.
- Seeing a word you know connect to a related one, or reappear inside a longer phrase, feels very close to how kanji and Chinese characters start to make sense once you know their parts.

## 3. Sethian

- **Developer / publisher:** Duang! Games
- **Released:** November 9, 2016 on Windows and macOS
- **Link:** [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/432370/Sethian/)

**The language mechanic.** You play an archaeologist investigating a long-vanished people. A computer they left behind may hold the answers you're looking for — but, as the Steam page puts it, it "only operates in the lost native language," so you have to learn that language to use it.

The Steam page calls its language, Sethianese, "a more earnest attempt at creating a fictitious language, using its own unique grammar and vocabulary," and says it borrows elements from Chinese, American Sign Language and other real languages. You keep a handwritten journal and a dictionary as you go.

**Why it's interesting for learners.**

- The language is the tool, not just the puzzle: you learn it because you need it to get something done. That is the most reliable motivation any real learner has.
- Because the grammar is its own, not a disguised English, it gives a taste of how different another language's word order and logic can feel.

## 4. Epigraph

- **Developer / publisher:** Matthew Brown (solo developer)
- **Released:** February 19, 2024 on Windows
- **Link:** [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2789770/Epigraph/)

**The language mechanic.** Its Steam page calls it "a short, challenging, language decipherment puzzle game." There's no story to wrap around the puzzle: you study inscriptions on seven artifacts, look for patterns, use the images to work out what symbols mean, cross-reference what you've learned, and type in your translations to uncover the secrets of the Nari.

**Why it's interesting for learners.**

- It is the purest version of the idea: no combat, no exploring, just you and a text in an unknown script. If the notebook parts of Chants of Sennaar were your favorite, this is that and nothing else.
- Spotting repeated patterns — the same ending on many words, the same symbol before every name — is exactly how you start noticing grammar in a real language before anyone explains it to you.

## 5. Tunic

- **Developer / publisher:** Isometricorp Games (Andrew Shouldice) / Finji
- **Released:** March 16, 2022 on Windows, macOS, Xbox One and Xbox Series X|S; Nintendo Switch, PlayStation 4 and PlayStation 5 on September 27, 2022
- **Link:** [Official site](https://tunicgame.com/)

**The language mechanic.** Tunic is an action adventure about a small fox, not a puzzle game first. But most of its text — including the in-game instruction manual you collect page by page — is written in a made-up script, and the game doesn't expect you to read it.

Players decided to read it anyway. Shortly after release, a Reddit user called oposdeo worked through around ten pages of the manual and showed that the script writes **English sounds** (phonemes), not English letters — as NME reported, they found that "of the 44 English phonemes," only two seemed unused. Shouldice has said the script is there to make the player "feel like they're in a place that they don't belong," and that if it meant anything, "it would be important that it not simply be a letter-to-letter cipher to English."

**Why it's interesting for learners.**

- It is a hands-on lesson in the difference between **spelling and sound**. English spelling hides the sounds; a script that writes sounds directly makes you hear words differently. That is the same insight behind learning the IPA, Korean Hangul, or Japanese kana.
- It shows how unreadable text turns into background noise until you decide to engage with it — the same thing that happens when you live abroad and stop "seeing" the signs.

## 6. Fez

- **Developer / publisher:** Polytron Corporation / Trapdoor
- **Released:** 2012 on Xbox 360, later Windows, macOS, Linux, PlayStation 3/4/Vita, iOS (2017) and Nintendo Switch (2021)
- **Link:** [Official site](https://fezgame.com/)

**The language mechanic.** Fez is a platformer in which you rotate between four 2D views of a 3D world to line up platforms. Underneath it is a layer of codes and glyphs, including a ciphered alphabet and its own number system, and the second half of the game is mostly about cracking them. Its hardest puzzle was famously solved by players working together online.

**Why it's interesting for learners.**

- Fez is about a _cipher_ — a disguised version of an alphabet you already know — rather than a new language. That makes it a useful contrast: learning a new script for a language you speak is a short puzzle, but learning a language is a long process. It is a reminder to not mistake "I can read the letters" for "I understand the language."
- Its community-solved puzzles show how much faster decipherment goes when learners share notes — the same reason study groups and shared decks help.

## Honorable mention: Outer Wilds

People often bring up Outer Wilds alongside these games because you spend much of it reading the writings of an alien people, the Nomai. But you read Nomai writing through an in-game translator tool rather than working out the script yourself, so it is a game about _reading_, not deciphering. Wonderful, but not a language puzzle.

## What these games teach about learning a real language

Playing several of these back to back, a few patterns stand out:

1. **Context first, definition second.** In every one of these games, you work out a word from where it appears before you ever get a translation. Real reading works the same way.
2. **A guess is not knowledge until it's tested.** Chants of Sennaar only accepts a word when you get a whole page right. In real learning, that test is _recall_ — can you remember what this means without looking?
3. **Meeting a word again is what makes it stick.** Heaven's Vault keeps bringing words back in new inscriptions. If you don't meet a word again, you forget it.

The last two are exactly what spaced repetition is for. Lianki uses the [FSRS algorithm](/en/blog/2025-01-15-fsrs-algorithm) to bring a word back just before you would forget it, so you don't have to wait for the game — or real life — to happen to show it to you again. And if you're reading real text in your target language online, the [Lianki userscript](/en/blog/2025-02-10-userscript) lets you save the page you're on as a card with `Alt+F`.

So: play Chants of Sennaar, enjoy the click of a page of glyphs finally making sense — and then try that same feeling on a real language.
