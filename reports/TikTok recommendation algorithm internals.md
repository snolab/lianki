# TikTok ranks predicted value, retrains by the minute

TikTok and Douyin run the same ByteDance recommender lineage: a recall, coarse-rank, fine-rank, dispersion pipeline whose fine-ranking score is a **sum over predicted behaviours of P(behaviour) x V(behaviour)**, with two of the four terms in the leaked 2021 formula being play-time terms and the stated optimisation targets being **retention and time spent** in service of daily active users ([NYT via Boston.com](https://www.boston.com/news/commentary/2021/12/06/how-tiktok-reads-your-mind)). Douyin's 2025 disclosure confirmed the same structure, now balancing **100+ objectives** under a "user long-term value" north star, and stated that the system almost no longer tags content or users at all; it predicts each user's probability of each behaviour on each item directly ([Xinhuanet](https://www.xinhuanet.com/tech/20250416/cb30d07cc64f497b938b82f551204e05/c.html)). The infrastructure underneath is Monolith: collisionless cuckoo-hash embedding tables, online training from a Kafka/Flink joined stream, and **minute-level sparse parameter sync** from training to serving parameter servers, dense weights nightly ([Monolith](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.07663)); by 2025 ByteDance had moved Douyin ranking onto GPU-resident, Transformer-like RankMixer/LONGER models scoring **10K-length user histories in roughly 14 ms** ([RankMixer](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.15551)). Independent audits agree that dwell time alone personalises a fresh account within **100 to 260 videos**, after which interest-aligned content fills 50 to 93 percent of the feed, and that diversity is enforced as a post-ranking constraint layer (no two consecutive videos from the same creator or sound, repetition limits on sensitive clusters) rather than inside the objective ([Baumann et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20231v1); [TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). For a language-learning feed the lesson is to copy the architecture and invert the objective: keep online per-card updates, staged item exposure, dispersion rules and user sliders, but rank by predicted learning value against a roughly 75 percent success target, treat reading time as a level signal only after normalising it by the card's expected time, cap over-long dwell rather than discarding the record, and make the Again/Hard/Good/Easy grade, never the swipe, the reward.

## Monolith syncs sparse weights every minute, dense weights nightly

The only end-to-end description ByteDance has published of its training and serving loop is the 2022 Monolith paper, and its two contributions explain why TikTok feels as if it reacts instantly. First, the embedding layer uses a **Cuckoo HashMap with two tables and two hash functions**, giving worst-case O(1) lookups and collision-free IDs for the billions of users and hundreds of millions of videos; IDs are admitted only after a tunable number of occurrences and **expire after a period of inactivity**, which keeps the multi-terabyte sparse model tractable ([Monolith](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.07663)). On MovieLens-25M, plain hashing collided on 7.73 percent of user IDs and 2.86 percent of movie IDs, and the collisionless table "consistently outperforms" it; on ByteDance's own production data with around **1,000 embedding tables** the collisionless model had higher AUC from the first epoch ([Monolith](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.07663)). Second, training is online: user actions and features stream through Kafka into a Flink "online joiner" that concatenates features with labels (with an in-memory cache and on-disk store for late-arriving labels), negative sampling is corrected with log-odds at serving time so the model stays unbiased, and the training parameter server pushes only **"touched keys" to the serving PS at minute granularity**, roughly 400 MB per minute for 100,000 updated IDs at dimension 1,024, while dense weights sync once a day at the traffic trough ([Monolith](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.07663)).

The paper is explicit that it trades reliability for freshness. Training servers snapshot only daily, with the argument that across 1,000 PS shards at a 0.01 percent failure rate the system loses "1 day's feedback from 15,000 users every 10 days," an acceptable cost against the gain from fresh weights ([Monolith](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.07663)). That gain is measured two ways. On Criteo, with five days of batch training and two of online training, a **30-minute sync interval reached 79.80 AUC versus 79.43 for batch**, and the curve flattens between one hour (79.78) and 30 minutes, which says minute-level sync is "good enough" rather than that sub-minute would help ([Monolith](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.07663)). In a seven-day live test on a production ads model the paper reports daily improvements of 14 to 18 percent, labelled "AUC improvement" but far larger than any absolute AUC delta, so it should be read as a relative online metric ([Monolith](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.07663)). The paper never says Monolith powers TikTok specifically, only "some of the most important products"; the TikTok attribution comes from press ([The Next Platform](https://www.nextplatform.com/2022/09/26/chinas-bytedance-intros-different-approach-to-recommendation-at-scale/)). Two further primary data points bound the feedback loop. Primus, ByteDance's batch-plus-stream training orchestrator deployed for five years, mixes Iceberg/HDFS history with Kafka streams and reports a 17.1 percent cluster training-cost reduction and 0.4 to 2.4 percent ad-revenue lift across four models ([Primus, ATC '25](https://www.usenix.org/conference/atc25/presentation/shan-jixi)). BytePlus Recommend, the commercial packaging, states that events sent via its Write APIs are "fed into the model after 10 seconds and reflected to the next recommendation call," with a default latency alert at 300 ms ([BytePlus docs](https://docs.byteplus.com/en/docs/recommend/resources-ways_to_improve_recommendation_performance); [BytePlus monitoring](https://docs.byteplus.com/en/docs/recommend/docs-api_monitoring_dashboard)). The 10-second figure is almost certainly real-time feature ingestion rather than weight updates, which supports the most useful architectural inference in these notes: the skip-and-it-adapts feeling inside a session comes from recent-behaviour sequence features computed at request time, while model weights change on a minute scale.

Since 2024 ByteDance has replaced the Monolith-era model internals, though not the online-training philosophy. Trinity (KDD '24) sits in retrieval and clusters items into enumerable groups to build "statistical interest histograms" that surface under-delivered long-term interests on Douyin ([Trinity](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02842)). RankMixer (KDD '25) argues that DCN/DHEN/FM-style feature-crossing modules "were inherited from the CPU era," swaps them for multi-head token mixing with per-token feed-forward blocks, and **raises model FLOPs utilisation from 4.5 to 45 percent, scaling the ranking model 100x in parameters at constant latency**: the 16M-parameter production baseline ran at 14.5 ms, the 1B-parameter RankMixer at 14.3 ms with fp16 and quantisation, on training data of "trillions of records per day" with 300-plus features; online it lifted active days 0.3 percent and in-app duration 1.08 percent, with low-activity users gaining most (+1.74 percent active days) ([RankMixer](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.15551)). LONGER (RecSys '25) scales user-sequence Transformers to 10 influential ByteDance scenarios and, crucially for the systems story, describes "fully synchronous" training and serving where **both dense and sparse parameters live on GPU machines, eliminating the external parameter server**, with HBM for hot features, CPU memory for warm, SSD for cold, and a KV cache that cuts serving throughput degradation from 40 percent to 6.8 percent ([LONGER](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04421)). The Douyin 10K-sequence paper adds linear-cost target-to-history cross attention and request-level batching so one user's encoding is shared across all candidates in a request, and reports "predictable, monotonic gains" with history length "mirroring the scaling law behaviour observed in large language models" ([arXiv 2511.06077](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.06077)). TokenMixer-Large (Feb 2026) reaches 7 billion parameters online and 15 billion offline ([TokenMixer-Large](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06563)). Multi-task heads are confirmed directly only for ByteDance short-video search, which "adopts the widely-used MMoE architecture to simultaneously learn multiple user behavior feedback" ([arXiv 2409.11281](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.11281)). What remains undisclosed is everything between these components: candidate counts per stage, the pre-ranking model, the final rerank, total request latency, and whether the international TikTok app runs the same 2025 models as Douyin; every online experiment in these papers is on Douyin, Douyin Ads or Douyin e-commerce.

## The score sums predicted behaviours times business value

The leaked "TikTok Algo 101" document, written by the Beijing engineering team for non-technical staff and confirmed as authentic by TikTok spokeswoman Hilary McQuaide, states the ranking score as **"Plike x Vlike + Pcomment x Vcomment + Eplaytime x Vplaytime + Pplay x Vplay"**, adds that "the actual equation in use is much more complicated, but the logic behind is the same," and names the goals as retention and time spent in pursuit of the "ultimate goal" of daily active users, under four headline values: user value, long-term user value, creator value, platform value ([NYT via Boston.com](https://www.boston.com/news/commentary/2021/12/06/how-tiktok-reads-your-mind)). The document described itself as the "same document for TikTok and Douyin," and the NYT found a Chinese Douyin document using the identical formula ([Pixel Envy](https://pxlnv.com/linklog/tiktok-leak-nyt/)). Four years later Douyin said the same thing in public: "comprehensive predicted user-behaviour probability x behaviour value weight = video recommendation priority," with predicted behaviours including completion, comment, like, share, duet and long-run consumption of an author, and engineer Liu Chang naming "user long-term value" as the north-star metric ([Wallstreetcn](https://wallstreetcn.com/articles/3745312); [Xinhuanet](https://www.xinhuanet.com/tech/20250416/cb30d07cc64f497b938b82f551204e05/c.html)). Douyin now claims to have "built and balanced **100+ objectives**" such as save, with dedicated models predicting save and re-watch probability, and composite long-horizon targets like "save + revisit," "follow + keep watching" and "open + search" ([CLS.cn](https://cls.cn/detail/1988109)). The shift from "DAU and time spent" to "long-term value" language followed a Cyberspace Administration campaign against "information cocoons" and reads as a reframing rather than a changed mechanism ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2025/04/01/bytedances-douyin-reveals-algorithm-amid-government-push-to-tackle-online-platform-issues/)). No source, leaked or official, gives numerical values for any V term.

Douyin's disclosure is also the only official map of the pipeline: recall, filtering and ranking stages, with coarse ranking that "generally does not use complex models" and fine ranking as "the most crucial part," built from collaborative filtering, two-tower retrieval that turns user and content features into comparable numerical "fingerprints," and Wide&Deep ranking ([The Paper](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_30665269); [TechNode](https://technode.com/2025/04/01/bytedances-douyin-reveals-algorithm-amid-government-push-to-tackle-online-platform-issues/)). The most striking claim is that the algorithm "almost no longer relies on tagging content or users" and operates "without understanding content," predicting target-behaviour probabilities directly from ID embeddings and behaviour histories ([CLS.cn](https://cls.cn/detail/1988109)). This is consistent with Monolith's thousand embedding tables and the 2025 sequence models: the "understanding" lives in collaborative structure, not taxonomy. Content classification survives only for safety: Douyin runs a first/second/third review ladder where "the more traffic a piece of content gets, the more times it is evaluated and the stricter the standard," with machines handling breadth and humans depth ([Xinhuanet](https://www.xinhuanet.com/tech/20250416/cb30d07cc64f497b938b82f551204e05/c.html)).

TikTok's own international description, unchanged in substance since 2020, lists three signal classes: user interactions (likes, shares, follows, comments, content created), video information (captions, sounds, hashtags), and device/account settings (language, country, device type), the last weighted lowest; it states that "whether a user finishes watching a longer video from beginning to end" is a strong indicator that outweighs a weak one like shared country, and that **neither follower count nor past high-performing videos are direct ranking factors** ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). Audits converge on implicit signals dominating. The WSJ's 100 bot accounts never liked or followed; they only lingered and rewatched, and TikTok identified their interests "in under two hours," some "in less than 40 minutes" ([Tubefilter/WSJ](https://www.tubefilter.com/2021/07/21/tiktok-recommendation-algorithm-wall-street-journal-guillaume-chaslot/)). Boeker and Urman's 2021 sock-puppet audit ranked follow (2.03 percent divergence per batch) above view rate (0.98) above like (0.82) against a 0.42 control ([arXiv 2201.12271](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12271)), while the 2025 Kempelen replication found watch duration, especially a 400 percent rewatch, the strongest factor and likes/follows weaker than in 2022, with the warning that changing the measurement metric "produced completely different findings" ([Mosnar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18140v1)). Read together with two play-time terms in a four-term formula, the right conclusion is that dwell is the dominant input and that explicit actions are inputs to the score rather than its goal.

Staged exposure for new uploads is the area where folklore outruns evidence. Creator-marketing sources claim every new video is tested on "somewhere between 200 and 350 people" and promoted on watch-to-end rate, and the "views stall at 300" plateau is widely reported ([Lilach Bullock](https://www.lilachbullock.com/why-tiktok-views-stop-at-300/)); none of this appears in any TikTok or Douyin document. What is confirmed is weaker but still informative: the WSJ bots were first served "very popular videos vetted by app moderators" averaging **6.31 million views, falling to 0.78 million** as the feed narrowed ([9to5mac/WSJ](https://9to5mac.com/2021/07/28/how-tiktoks-algorithm-works/)), and Douyin's escalating review ladder implies traffic tiers exist internally. Because Monolith trains online with minute-level sync, a new video's first impressions necessarily move its predicted scores within minutes, so a graduated process is real in some form; the specific tier sizes are unverified. Two further leaks colour the objective. Unredacted portions of the Kentucky attorney general's complaint quote internal research that "all it takes to get addicted is 260 videos," and state TikTok "retooled its algorithm to amplify users that the company deemed beautiful" after finding too many "unattractive subjects" in the main feed ([NPR via KVCR](https://www.kvcrnews.org/2024-10-11/the-biggest-findings-from-uncensored-tiktok-lawsuit-documents)). And the US app has become a fork: under the January 2026 joint venture, Oracle "recreates and retrains the algorithm 'from the ground up'" on US data under licence from ByteDance ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/409287/oracle-to-recreate-tiktoks-algorithm-under-new-de.html); [AP via ABC7](https://abc7news.com/post/oracle-will-manage-tiktoks-algorithm-us-users-trump-administration-deal/17866333/)), so findings about the global model will diverge from the US one over time.

## Personalisation locks in within 100 to 260 videos

Cold start is an optional interest-category picker ("pets or travel") that falls back to "a generalized feed of popular videos" for users who decline ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)), and every audit since finds the feed reshaped within one session. The WSJ's "kentucky_96" bot rewatched one sadness video after 15 videos and reached a feed that was **93 percent depression content after 224 videos and 33 minutes**, with TikTok attributing the remaining 7 percent to discovery ([Tubefilter/WSJ](https://www.tubefilter.com/2021/07/21/tiktok-recommendation-algorithm-wall-street-journal-guillaume-chaslot/)). Vombatkere and colleagues, using 347 real users' GDPR data (9.2 million views) plus bots, found exploit fraction rising over the first videos and stabilising "after recommendation index 100," with real users at 50 percent or more exploit versus 31 percent for bots and 20 percent for a randomised baseline; drivers ranked follow (p = 1e-14) over like (p = 1e-5) over watch percentage (p = 0.03), with early skip rate not significant ([arXiv 2403.12410](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.12410)). Baumann and colleagues' 42 bots over roughly 3,000 videos each saw interest-aligned share reach 67.4 percent for gaming and 52.3 percent for food, with "most onset points within the first 200 videos" (means of 66 to 140 videos, about 1.5 hours), and hashtag diversity correlating at -0.92 with amplification ([arXiv 2503.20231](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20231v1)). Amnesty's 13-year-old personas saw "almost 1 in 2 videos" mental-health related after five to six hours ([Amnesty](https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/POL40/7350/2023/en/)), and TikTok's own leaked threshold is **260 videos, under 35 minutes**, with an employee entering "negative filter bubbles" within 20 minutes ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2024/10/11/g-s1-27676/tiktok-redacted-documents-in-teen-safety-lawsuit-revealed)). A planning number of "personalisation visible within 100 to 250 items" is therefore well supported, and real users with richer signals personalise faster than bots.

Exploration is framed by TikTok as pattern interruption, not a traffic budget. The 2020 post says the system "intersperses diverse types of content" and surfaces videos that "don't appear to be relevant to your expressed interests," and "generally won't show two videos in a row made with the same sound or by the same creator" ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you)). The leaked document adds penalty terms such as **"same_author_seen" and "same_tag_today"** plus "forced recommendation in users' for u feed and dispersion" ([NYT via Boston.com](https://www.boston.com/news/commentary/2021/12/06/how-tiktok-reads-your-mind)), which is the same post-scoring re-ranking that Douyin calls "diversity scattering," alongside multi-interest recall, niche-interest support, random recommendations and social-relationship discovery ([Xinhuanet](https://www.xinhuanet.com/tech/20250416/cb30d07cc64f497b938b82f551204e05/c.html)). From December 2021 TikTok tested, and by July 2022 shipped, repetition limits on clusters that "may be fine as a single video but problematic if viewed in clusters" (extreme dieting, fitness, sadness, breakups), and by March 2023 said it "identifies repetitive themes and substitutes them with videos about other topics" ([TikTok Newsroom, Dec 2021](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/an-update-on-our-work-to-safeguard-and-diversify-recommendations); [TikTok Newsroom, July 2022](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/ways-for-our-community-to-enjoy-what-they-love?lang=en-IE); [TikTok Newsroom, March 2023](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-ie/safeguarding-recommendations-update)). The Kempelen replication observed "an exploration phase at the start, followed by strong exploitation after interacting with enough videos (around 1000)" and cross-bot hashtag overlap of about 10 percent versus 35 percent in 2022, which it reads as "a strong exploration shift" ([Mosnar et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.18140v1)). No source quantifies the explore share, candidate-pool size or dispersion window.

User controls are mostly suppressive. As of 2026 the set is Not Interested (hide creator or sound), keyword and hashtag filters now AI-expanded to synonyms with a 200-keyword cap, **Manage Topics sliders for 10-plus topics that "won't eliminate any topics entirely, but can influence how often they're recommended,"** Refresh (view the feed "as if they just signed up" while keeping filters and follows), the STEM feed on by default in the US, UK, Ireland and Canada with content vetted by Common Sense Networks and Poynter, Friends and Following feeds, and in the EEA a non-personalised feed of locally and globally popular videos ([TikTok Newsroom, June 2025](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/more-ways-to-discover-new-content-and-creators-you-love); [TikTok Newsroom, March 2023](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-ie/safeguarding-recommendations-update); [TikTok Newsroom, Nov 2024](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/tiktok-stem-feed-now-available-to-all-users-by-default?lang=en-GB); [TikTok Newsroom EU](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-eu/compliance-digital-services-act-eu)). Session controls fare worse. Under-18s get a default 60-minute daily limit with a passcode to continue ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/next/2023/03/02/tiktok-sets-60-minute-screen-time-limit-for-users-under-18-and-a-sleep-reminder-for-all)), but TikTok's internal measurement found it **cut teen usage from about 108.5 to 107 minutes a day**, a project manager wrote "Our goal is not to reduce the time spent," and success was measured by "improving public trust via media coverage" ([KPR/NPR](https://www.kaxe.org/news/2024-10-11/tiktok-executives-know-about-apps-effect-on-teens-lawsuit-documents-allege)). The European Commission's February 2026 preliminary finding names the personalised recommender together with infinite scroll, autoplay and push notifications as addictive design, calls the time tools "easy to dismiss," and suggests disabling infinite scroll over time and nighttime restrictions, with fines up to 6 percent of global turnover ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-preliminarily-finds-tiktoks-addictive-design-breach-digital-services-act)). China got there first by statute: the March 2022 algorithm provisions require an opt-out from personalisation, user-tag management and a ban on models "leading users to addiction," and Douyin's youth mode caps under-14s at 40 minutes a day with a 10 pm to 6 am lockout ([DigiChina](https://digichina.stanford.edu/work/translation-internet-information-service-algorithmic-recommendation-management-provisions-effective-march-1-2022/); [CNN via WTOP](https://wtop.com/tech/2021/09/the-chinese-version-of-tiktok-is-limiting-kids-to-40-minutes-a-day/)).

## A learning feed should copy the loop and invert the objective

The architectural ideas transfer almost one for one, because a learner's card stream has the same shape as a video stream: a per-user state updated after every item, a multi-stage selection from a candidate pool, and a diversity layer on top. The objective does not transfer, and the evidence on short-form learning says why. In a randomised comparison of four one-minute videos against one eight-minute video with identical content, short form "produced higher engagement and satisfaction ratings" but retention on immediate and one-week tests was **not statistically different** ([McKeown 2026](https://library.iated.org/view/MCKEOWN2026DES)); when the same documentary was instead cut into clips interspersed with non-informative filler, viewers scored lower immediately and "forgot a much higher percentage of their initial knowledge overnight," with fMRI synchrony confined to "immediate, automatic auditory processing" regions ([Wei et al. 2026 via PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/the-illusion-of-learning-from-short-videos/)). Segmentation is fine; fragmentation is not. A meta-analysis of 71 studies and 98,299 participants associates heavier short-form use with poorer attention (r = -.38) and inhibitory control (r = -.41), with causality unresolved ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/)). And Bjork's desirable-difficulties work holds that "current performance is an unreliable index of learning": the fluent, fast, satisfying sessions a watch-time objective would select for are precisely the ones that under-deliver retention ([Bjork & Bjork](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_inpress.pdf)). A feed that ranked cards by Eplaytime would reproduce TikTok's rabbit hole with vocabulary the learner already knows.

Every production learning system that works replaces the engagement objective with an explicit difficulty or retention target. Duolingo's Birdbrain V1 is "a flavor of logistic regression inspired by item response theory" that models P(correct) as a function of exercise difficulty (a sum of component features such as exercise type and vocabulary) and learner ability, updates by one step of SGD and "generalizes the Elo rating system"; V2 is an LSTM that compresses the interaction history into 40 numbers and updates "within minutes of a learner completing an exercise," and Duolingo A/B-tests it on "both engagement and learning measures" ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)). Pelánek's Elo formulation for adaptive practice uses an uncertainty function U(n) = a/(1 + bn) so estimates are coarse after a few answers and stable later, and "uses a target success rate (e.g., 75%)" ([Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)); FSRS exposes desired retention with 70 to 97 percent as the reasonable band ([ABC of FSRS](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/ABC-of-FSRS)); Krashen's i+1 says the same in pedagogical terms ([Krashen](http://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf)). Duolingo also separates the two questions TikTok collapses into one score: half-life regression (memory half-life 2^(theta x features) over lag time, correct and incorrect counts and per-lexeme indicators) decides when to resurface an item, reaching 0.13 MAE, "nearly half that of the Leitner system," and lifting activity 12 percent in A/B tests, while Birdbrain decides what difficulty to serve next ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn); [Settles & Meeder 2016](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/P16-1174.pdf)). The Lianki feed already has FSRS for the "when"; it needs an Elo/IRT ability scalar for the "what."

The table below maps each confirmed TikTok mechanism onto its learning-feed analogue, with the inversion marked where the objective flips.

| TikTok/Douyin mechanism (confirmed) | Learning-feed analogue | Keep or invert |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Score = sum of P(behaviour) x V(behaviour), play-time dominant | Score = predicted learning value: closeness of P(recall) to the ~75% target, novelty of target vocabulary, FSRS due-ness | Invert: dwell is a feature, never a reward |
| Minute-level online sparse updates; real-time sequence features at request time | Update the learner's Elo ability and FSRS state synchronously after every grade; feed the last N grades and time ratios as request-time features | Keep |
| Recall, coarse rank, fine rank, dispersion | Generate or retrieve a candidate pool at ability plus delta, filter by CEFR/vocabulary checks, rank by learning value, then disperse | Keep |
| Item cold start: new video scored from first impressions; follower count ignored | A freshly generated card has no history: seed difficulty from generation parameters (target CEFR, frequency band, sentence count), as Birdbrain V1 sums component features | Keep, with priors doing the work |
| No two consecutive videos from the same creator or sound | No two consecutive cards drilling the same word or pattern; vary surface form inside a short story arc | Keep at the skill level, not the topic level |
| Repetition limits on sensitive clusters (sadness, dieting) | Cap consecutive Again grades; bound per-session ability drift to one sub-band to break frustration or boredom loops | Keep |
| Interest picker, then personalisation within 100 to 260 items | Placement picker, then Elo with decaying K converges in tens of cards | Keep |
| Manage Topics sliders adjust frequency, not exclusion; Refresh resets | Topic sliders and a level reset, both frequency-based | Keep |
| 60-minute soft limit cut usage by 1.5 min/day | Hard session ends and spacing are the point, not a nuisance: Cepeda's 254-study meta-analysis shows distributed practice beats massed ([Cepeda et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)) | Invert |

Two more design constraints come from the content side. Cards should run in **short thematic arcs of three to five** (same story, character or grammar target) so that adjacent swipes are segmentation rather than fragmentation, with interleaving of surface form inside the arc; this is the direct reading of the McKeown versus Wei contrast. And the generator must be level-checked, because LLMs "generate text at a near-native level of complexity": in a study with university learners of Japanese, prompting alone failed while controllable-generation techniques raised beginner comprehensibility from 39.4 to 83.3 percent ([arXiv 2506.04072](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04072v1)), so each card needs automatic readability and vocabulary-frequency validation against its target band before it is served ([arXiv 2409.20246](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.20246)).

## Reading time is a level signal only after normalisation, and the 60-second rule should cap, not drop

The notes make a strong case that raw reading time is almost useless and normalised reading time is valuable. Brysbaert's meta-analysis of 190 studies puts adult L1 silent reading at **238 wpm for English nonfiction** (most adults 175 to 300), 260 wpm for Chinese using a 1.5-characters-per-word conversion (about 390 characters per minute), and only about 100 wpm when reading for recall, with short-passage studies spanning just over 100 to nearly 400 wpm ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)). The between-study spread is wider than any between-level difference, so an absolute threshold cannot classify a learner's level. Two findings do transfer. First, **time per matched sentence is roughly invariant across scripts**: English, Finnish and Mandarin texts took the same time per sentence despite 1.5 characters per word in Chinese versus 5.6 letters in English, and the same held for Hebrew ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf)). Expected card time should therefore be computed from sentence and proposition count, not raw characters, which also makes the rule portable across Lianki's target languages. Second, the L2 penalty is known in order of magnitude: proficient bilinguals read 10 to 17 percent slower in L2, "most L2 learners read slowly, at one-half to one-third the reading rate by L1 readers," with an L2 French group moving from 79 to 101 wpm after training ([Brysbaert 2019](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf); [PMC6602001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6602001/table/T1)), and Shen's L2 Chinese benchmarks run 300 to 499 characters per minute in year four and over 500 in year five ([University of Hawaii](https://manoa.hawaii.edu/ccle/2023/01/06/ccle-talk-series-professor-helen-shen-and-chinese-l2-reading-proficiency-building)). No CEFR-, JLPT- or HSK-indexed reading-rate table exists. For a 60-second card budget this implies an L2 reader at roughly half L1 speed handles 100 to 130 English words or 150 to 250 CJK characters a minute, and a card meant to be read with comprehension at the recall-reading rate should hold **about 40 to 80 L2 words or 80 to 150 CJK characters for a 30 to 45 second target**.

The signal to feed the level estimator is the ratio of observed to expected time, with the learner's own expected rate learned online as a running median over recent Good-graded cards, and it should be combined with the grade the way Pelánek describes Klinkenberg's "high speed, high stakes" rule: a fast correct answer is strong evidence of ability, a slow correct answer is weak evidence, and "with increasing response time the score goes towards zero (for both correct and incorrect answers)" as it approaches the time limit ([Pelánek 2016](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/CAE-elo.pdf)). Math Garden uses accuracy and response time together with a dynamic K-factor ([Tilburg](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/balancing-stability-and-flexibility-investigating-a-dynamic-k-val/)), while Anki records time for statistics only and FSRS ignores it for scheduling ([Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)), which is the right split for Lianki too: time ratio updates the ability estimate that picks the next card's difficulty; it never touches the memory model that decides when a known item returns. Kuaishou's duration-bias work is the warning: watch-time prediction "is always biased towards videos with long duration," and without deconfounding a feed drifts toward longer items ([Zhan et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.06003)). Unnormalised reading time would drift the generator toward longer cards in exactly the same way.

The 60-second cap is an Anki convention, not a finding. Anki's "Maximum answer seconds" defaults to 60 because "Anki assumes you have walked away from your computer or have been distracted, and caps the recorded time at 60 seconds," and separately "the time taken does not influence scheduling" ([Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)). The learning-analytics literature shows that the choice of idle threshold materially changes which predictors look significant ([Kovanović et al. 2015](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/penetrating-the-black-box-of-time-on-task-estimation/)), and a log-normal mixture over on-task and off-task gaps estimates time-on-task without any fixed cutoff ([arXiv 1805.01819](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01819)). The proposed design, dropping records over 60 seconds, discards too much: a learner who sat with a hard card for 70 seconds and then pressed Again has given a valid grade. Duolingo's precedent points the other way; it streams partial lesson data precisely because "roughly 20 percent of lessons started on Duolingo aren't completed," treating abandonment as data ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/duolingo)). The better rule is content-aware: cap at a multiple of the card's expected time (around three times, with a floor near 20 to 30 seconds and 60 seconds as the absolute ceiling for sub-minute cards), **keep the grade and drop only the time signal when the cap is hit**, store the raw and capped values so the threshold can be re-evaluated offline, and treat visibility and focus-loss events (tab hidden, app backgrounded, no touch for N seconds) as stronger idle evidence than any dwell number. A capped time must never count as "slow reader" evidence in the ability estimator.

Swipe-to-grade is where the TikTok evidence cuts hardest. The WSJ showed that linger time alone, with no likes or follows, personalised a feed to 93 percent of one topic in 33 minutes ([Tubefilter/WSJ](https://www.tubefilter.com/2021/07/21/tiktok-recommendation-algorithm-wall-street-journal-guillaume-chaslot/)); dwell is that powerful as a reward, which is exactly why it must not be one here. Short dwell is ambiguous ("short time might actually equal satisfaction" in some contexts) ([arXiv 2012.13992](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2012.13992)), and lingering without interest, "tolerance," predicts lower future activity; separating it from interest improved retention in a field study ([arXiv 2412.09950](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09950)). Interspersing retrieval questions inside short instructional videos improves both short- and long-term recall ([Sydney IISME](https://openjournals.library.sydney.edu.au/IISME/article/view/21175)), so the grade itself is the learning event, not just a label. The design rules that follow: require the Again/Hard/Good/Easy grade (or an equivalent cloze or micro-question) before the card advances; treat an ungraded swipe-away as Again or as missing data, never as a neutral or positive event; enforce a minimum exposure before a skip is honoured, which both blunts the attention-training effect of fast swiping and keeps the time ratio meaningful; and A/B test on next-day and one-week recall, logging session length alongside so the trade-off stays visible, as Duolingo does. Short-session app learning does produce measurable proficiency when the difficulty target is enforced: 245 learners who completed Duolingo's A2 English content scored ACTFL Intermediate High in reading and listening, one sublevel above expectation ([Jiang et al. 2024](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-duolingo-english-courses-in-developing-readi/)). No equivalent randomised study of a swipe-based language feed with delayed outcomes exists yet, so Lianki would be generating that evidence rather than consuming it.

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing from this research is that TikTok's power comes from two separable things, an architecture and an objective, and only the first is worth copying. The architecture is a per-user state that updates within minutes, a multi-stage selector that scores ID-level collaborative signal without understanding content, and a constraint layer that enforces diversity after scoring. The objective is Eplaytime x Vplaytime with retention as the metric, and every independent audit, the leaked 260-video threshold, and the 1.5-minute effect of TikTok's own time limit show that this objective is both very effective and not steerable by soft controls once it is in place. A learning feed inherits the attention cost of the swipe interface, the infinite-scroll liability the European Commission is now pursuing, and the fragmentation effect on overnight recall unless it deliberately designs against them; the engineering is easy, the restraint is the product decision.

For Lianki specifically the design collapses to a small set of commitments: two models rather than one (an Elo/IRT ability scalar for what to generate, FSRS for when to resurface), expected time computed from sentence count rather than characters so the time ratio is script-invariant across languages, caps that preserve grades and discard only time, cards generated in short coherent arcs and validated against a CEFR band before serving, dispersion applied at the word-and-pattern level rather than the topic level, and evaluation on delayed recall. The open evidence gap is real: nobody has published a randomised study of a swipe-graded language feed with delayed vocabulary outcomes, and the reading-rate literature has no CEFR-indexed norms, so the first production weeks should be instrumented to learn the per-learner expected-rate distribution and the delayed-recall effect of arc length, since those two numbers, not any TikTok parameter, will decide whether the feed teaches.
